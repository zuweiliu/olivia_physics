Section 1 of 6 — Core Concepts

Centripetal
Force

🔗 Prerequisite: Circular Motion Kinematics

This lesson builds directly on the Circular Motion Kinematics lesson. You learned that an object moving in a circle has centripetal acceleration \(a_c = v^2/r\) pointing toward the center. Now we ask: what force causes that acceleration?

You're in a car making a sharp turn. You feel pushed outward against the door. But here's the twist — there's actually a force pushing you inward. That's the centripetal force, and without it, you'd fly off in a straight line (Newton's 1st Law!).

🎮 The red arrow shows the centripetal force — always pulling toward the center. The green arrow is velocity (tangent).

There is no such thing as "centrifugal force" in physics! What you feel as being thrown outward is just your body wanting to go straight (inertia) while the car turns underneath you. The real force is always inward.

💡 The Big Idea — Newton's 2nd Law Meets the Circle

From the previous lesson: \(a_c = v^2/r\). Newton's 2nd Law says \(F = ma\). Combine them:

\[ F_c = m\,a_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]

This is the net force toward the center required to keep an object moving in a circle. It's not a new type of force — it's just Newton's 2nd Law applied to circular motion!

Centripetal Force: \(F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\)

Where it comes from: We already know \(a_c = v^2/r\) (from kinematics). Newton's 2nd Law says the net force in any direction equals \(ma\). In the radial direction (toward center):

\[ F_c = ma_c = m\frac{v^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r \]

🔍 Formula Analysis: \(F_c = mv^2/r\)

VariableRolePhysics Meaning of Change
\(F_c\)Net inward force (N)Total force pointing toward center
\(m\)Mass — constantHeavier object needs more force for same circle
\(v\)Speed — can varyDouble \(v\) → 4× the force needed!
\(r\)Radius — constant for a given pathTighter curve (smaller \(r\)) → more force needed
⚠️ Critical Understanding

Centripetal force is not a new force. It's the name for whatever net force happens to point toward the center of the circle. It could be tension, gravity, friction, a normal force component, or a combination!

🔑 The Force Analysis Recipe (Use This EVERY Time)

Force analysis is the core skill of this lesson. Before you touch any formula, follow these four steps:

1
Draw a Free Body Diagram (FBD)

Isolate the object. Draw only forces exerted ON it by other objects. Ask: what is touching it? What field forces (gravity) act on it? Every force needs a source!

2
Choose your coordinate axes

For circular motion: one axis points toward the center (radial), the other is perpendicular to it. Decompose every force into these two directions.

3
Write Newton's 2nd Law for EACH axis

Toward center: \(\sum F_{\text{radial}} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\)  (net inward force = centripetal force)
Perpendicular: \(\sum F_{\perp} = 0\)  (no acceleration in this direction, so forces balance)

4
Solve for the unknown

Use the known forces (weight \(mg\), angles, friction \(\mu_s N\)) and the two equations to find the unknown force or speed.

🚨 The #1 Mistake Students Make

Students often write \(F_c = mv^2/r\) as if centripetal force is a separate force to add to the diagram. It is NOT.

\(\dfrac{mv^2}{r}\) is the result — it equals the net force toward the center, which is the sum of all the real forces (tension, gravity, friction, normal force) in the radial direction. Never draw "\(F_c\)" on an FBD!

Scenario 1: Ball on a String 🪢 (Horizontal Circle)

A ball of mass \(m\) swings in a horizontal circle on a string of length \(r\).

Step 1: What forces act ON the ball?

Contact forces:

What touches the ball? The string → Tension \(T\), directed along the string toward the center.

Field forces:

Gravity always acts → Weight \(mg\), straight down.

That's it! Only 2 forces. No "centripetal force" arrow. No "centrifugal force."

Step 2 & 3: Choose axes → Write Newton's 2nd Law

For a perfectly horizontal circle (simplified): tension is horizontal, weight is vertical.

\[ \text{Toward center (radial): } T = \frac{mv^2}{r} \] \[ \text{Vertical: balanced (string is horizontal)} \]

The tension IS the net radial force. Only tension points toward the center, so tension = centripetal force.

Step 4: Solve

Known: \(m\), \(v\), \(r\) → Unknown: \(T\)
\(T = mv^2/r\). That's the tension the string must provide!

Scenario 2: Car on a Flat Curve 🚗

A car of mass \(m\) rounds a flat (level) curve of radius \(r\) at speed \(v\).

Step 1: What forces act ON the car?

Contact forces (what touches the car?):

• Road surface → Normal force \(N\) (perpendicular to road = straight up)
• Road surface → Static friction \(f_s\) (parallel to road, pointing toward the center of the turn)

Field forces:

• Weight \(mg\), straight down.

3 forces total. That's it.

Step 2 & 3: Choose axes → Newton's 2nd Law

Radial = horizontal toward center. Perpendicular = vertical.

\[ \textbf{Vertical (no acceleration):}\quad N - mg = 0 \implies \boxed{N = mg} \] \[ \textbf{Toward center:}\quad f_s = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]

Only friction points toward the center → friction IS the net radial force → friction = centripetal force.
\(N\) and \(mg\) are both vertical — they don't contribute to the radial direction at all.

Step 4: Solve — What's the max speed before skidding?

Friction has a maximum: \(f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N\). We already know \(N = mg\) from the vertical equation!

\[ f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = \mu_s mg \] \[ \text{Set max friction = centripetal force:} \quad \mu_s mg = \frac{mv^2_{\max}}{r} \] \[ \text{Cancel } m \text{ from both sides:} \quad \mu_s g = \frac{v^2_{\max}}{r} \] \[ \boxed{v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}} \]

See how we used the vertical equation (\(N = mg\)) inside the radial equation? That's the power of writing both equations. The "perpendicular" equation often gives you a known force to substitute!

✅ Why does mass cancel?

On the left, friction depends on \(m\) (through \(N = mg\)). On the right, centripetal force also depends on \(m\). Since both sides are proportional to \(m\), it cancels. A truck and a motorcycle have the same max speed on the same curve (assuming same tires).

💡 Big Idea #2 — Banked Curves

What if we tilt the road? On a banked curve, the normal force is no longer vertical — it's tilted. Its horizontal component points toward the center, providing centripetal force without any friction!

Scenario 3: Banked Curve (No Friction) ✈️

A car of mass \(m\) moves at speed \(v\) on a curve banked at angle \(\theta\). No friction.

Step 1: What forces act ON the car?

Contact forces:

• Normal force \(N\) from the road — perpendicular to the tilted surface (so \(N\) is tilted too!)
• No friction (the problem says so).

Field forces:

• Weight \(mg\), straight down.

Only 2 forces! But \(N\) is tilted — that changes everything.

Step 2: Choose axes — this is the key step!

We use horizontal (toward center) and vertical (up/down) — NOT along the tilted surface.

Because the acceleration is horizontal (toward the center of the circle), we decompose \(N\) into horizontal and vertical components:

Horizontal component of \(N\)

\(N\sin\theta\)

Points toward the center → provides centripetal force!

Vertical component of \(N\)

\(N\cos\theta\)

Points up → must balance weight \(mg\)

Step 3: Newton's 2nd Law for each axis

\[ \textbf{Vertical (no vertical acceleration):}\quad N\cos\theta = mg \quad \cdots\text{(Eq. 1)} \] \[ \textbf{Toward center:}\quad N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad \cdots\text{(Eq. 2)} \]

Step 4: Solve — use known force to eliminate unknown!

The trick: We have two equations and two unknowns (\(N\) and \(v\), or \(N\) and \(\theta\)). Divide Eq. 2 by Eq. 1 to eliminate \(N\):

\[ \frac{N\sin\theta}{N\cos\theta} = \frac{mv^2/r}{mg} \] \[ \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg} \] \[ \boxed{v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}} \]

Notice: both \(N\) and \(m\) cancel! The ideal speed on a banked curve doesn't depend on the car's mass or the normal force. It only depends on the geometry (\(r\), \(\theta\)) and gravity (\(g\)).

🔑 Why divide the equations? — A general technique

Whenever you have two equations that share an unknown force (here, \(N\)), dividing one by the other eliminates it. This "divide trick" works any time a tilted force appears in both equations — you'll see it again with banking planes!

Scenario 4: Vertical Circle — Bottom 🎢

An object of mass \(m\) moves in a vertical circle of radius \(r\). Let's analyze forces at the bottom (e.g., bottom of a roller coaster loop, a monkey swinging past the lowest point).

Step 1: What forces act ON the object at the bottom?

Contact force:

• Tension \(T\) (or Normal force \(N\)) — directed upward along the string/track (toward the center of the circle, which is above the object).

Field force:

• Weight \(mg\) — directed downward (away from the center).

Key observation: \(T\) and \(mg\) point in opposite directions! Tension is toward center, weight is away.

Step 2 & 3: The radial direction is vertical (upward = toward center)

\[ \textbf{Toward center (up):}\quad T - mg = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]

Read this equation carefully:
• \(T\) is positive (it points toward center ✅)
• \(mg\) is subtracted (it points away from center ❌)
• The net of these two = \(mv^2/r\)

Step 4: Solve for the unknown tension

\[ \boxed{T = mg + \frac{mv^2}{r}} \]

Physics meaning: The tension must do two jobs:
① Support the weight (\(mg\)) — just to hold the object up
② Provide extra inward force (\(mv^2/r\)) — to curve the path
So \(T > mg\) always! You feel heavier at the bottom of a loop.

✅ Example numbers: A 60 kg person at the bottom of a roller coaster loop (\(r = 10\) m, \(v = 12\) m/s):

\(N = mg + mv^2/r = 60(9.80) + 60(144)/10 = 588 + 864 = \mathbf{1452\ N}\).
That's 2.5× their normal weight — they feel 2.5g! 🎢

Scenario 5: Vertical Circle — Top 🎢

Same object, now at the top of the vertical circle.

Step 1: What forces act ON the object at the top?

Contact force:

• \(N\) (or \(T\)) — directed downward (toward center, which is below the object now).

Field force:

• Weight \(mg\) — directed downward (also toward center!).

Key observation: At the top, BOTH forces point toward the center (both downward)! They work together.

Step 2 & 3: The radial direction is vertical (downward = toward center)

\[ \textbf{Toward center (down):}\quad mg + N = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]

Both \(mg\) and \(N\) are positive (both point toward center).
They add up to equal the centripetal force.

Step 4: Solve for the unknown normal force

\[ \boxed{N = \frac{mv^2}{r} - mg} \]

Physics meaning: Gravity already provides \(mg\) toward center. You only need \(N\) for the remaining centripetal force. Since \(N = mv^2/r - mg\):
• If \(v\) is large → \(N > 0\) (track pushes down on you, you feel lighter than normal)
• If \(v\) is just right → \(N = 0\) (you feel weightless! gravity alone provides all the centripetal force)
• If \(v\) is too small → formula gives \(N < 0\), which is impossible — you lose contact and fall!

⚠️ Minimum speed at the top

Set \(N = 0\) (barely maintaining contact):
\(mg = mv^2_{\min}/r\) → \(\boxed{v_{\min} = \sqrt{gr}}\).
Below this speed, the object falls away from the circular path. This is why roller coasters must go fast enough at the top!

📊 Compare All Scenarios Side by Side

Look at the pattern: it's always the same recipe, just different forces.

Section 3 of 6 — Worked Example

Example: Jet Banking Turn ✈️

Problem Statement

A jet flying at 123 m/s banks to make a horizontal circular turn. The radius of the turn is 3810 m, and the mass of the jet is 2.0 × 10⁵ kg. Calculate the magnitude of the necessary lifting force.

Work through each step by clicking the buttons below 👇

1
Draw the Free Body Diagram

What forces act on the banking jet?

Forces on the banked jet:
- Lift \(L\): perpendicular to wings (tilted at angle \(\theta\))
- Weight \(mg\): straight down

The horizontal component of lift (\(L\sin\theta\)) provides centripetal force.
The vertical component of lift (\(L\cos\theta\)) balances weight.

\[ \text{Horizontal: } L\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r} \] \[ \text{Vertical: } L\cos\theta = mg \]
2
Solve for the lift force \(L\)

Use both equations together

Square both equations and add them (since \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\)):

\[ L^2\sin^2\theta + L^2\cos^2\theta = \left(\frac{mv^2}{r}\right)^2 + (mg)^2 \] \[ L^2 = m^2\left(\frac{v^4}{r^2} + g^2\right) \] \[ L = m\sqrt{\frac{v^4}{r^2} + g^2} \]
3
Plug in the numbers

Calculate the final answer

\[ \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(123)^2}{3810} = \frac{15129}{3810} = 3.97\ m/s^2 \] \[ L = 2.0\times10^5 \times \sqrt{(3.97)^2 + (9.80)^2} \] \[ L = 2.0\times10^5 \times \sqrt{15.76 + 96.04} \] \[ L = 2.0\times10^5 \times \sqrt{111.8} = 2.0\times10^5 \times 10.57 \] \[ \boxed{L \approx 2.11 \times 10^6\ N} \]
✅ The lift force is about 2.11 million Newtons!

Compare to the jet's weight: \(mg = 2.0\times10^5 \times 9.80 = 1.96\times10^6\) N. The lift is only slightly more than the weight — this is a gentle turn (the centripetal acceleration is just 0.4g).

🤔 Thinking Question

If the jet needed to make a tighter turn (smaller \(r\)) at the same speed, would it need more or less lift force?

More lift! Smaller \(r\) → larger \(v^2/r\) → larger centripetal force needed → the jet must bank more steeply, increasing the required lift.

Problem: Amusement Park Ride

At an amusement park, a cylindrically shaped chamber spins around a central axis. People sit in seats with their backs against the outer wall. The outer wall moves at a speed of 3.2 m/s, and an 83 kg person feels a 560 N force pressing against his back.

(a) What is the radius of the chamber?
(b) What is the centripetal acceleration?

Part (a): Find the Radius

The 560 N force is the centripetal force (the wall pushes the person inward). Use \(F_c = mv^2/r\) and solve for \(r\).

Part (b): Centripetal Acceleration

Use \(a_c = v^2/r\) or \(a_c = F_c/m\).

🎉 Practice Complete!

The chamber has a radius of about 1.52 m and the rider feels about 6.75 m/s² of centripetal acceleration — roughly 0.69g. Enough to feel pressed into your seat!

Homework Problems 📝

Work through these problems on paper. Draw a free body diagram for each one!

📋 Key Formulas Reference

\(F_c = mv^2/r = m\omega^2 r\)  |  \(a_c = v^2/r\)  |  \(v = r\omega = 2\pi r/T\)
Flat curve: \(f_s = mv^2/r\), \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}\)
Banked curve: \(\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)\)

Problem 1

A 9.5 kg monkey hangs by one arm from a branch and swings on a vertical circle. The radial distance is 85 cm. At the lowest point, the monkey's speed is 2.8 m/s. (a) Find the centripetal force. (b) Find the tension in the monkey's arm.

(a) \(F_c = mv^2/r = 9.5 \times (2.8)^2 / 0.85 = \boxed{87.5\ N}\)
(b) At the bottom: \(T - mg = mv^2/r\), so \(T = mg + mv^2/r = 9.5(9.80) + 87.5 = 93.1 + 87.5 = \boxed{180.6\ N}\)
Problem 2

On a banked race track, the smallest circular path has radius 112 m and the largest has radius 165 m. The height of the outer wall is 18 m. Find (a) the smallest and (b) the largest speed at which cars can move without relying on friction.

Bank angle: \(\sin\theta = 18/(165-112) = 18/53\), so \(\theta = 19.8°\), \(\tan\theta = 0.360\).
(a) \(v_{\min} = \sqrt{r_{\min} \cdot g \cdot \tan\theta} = \sqrt{112 \times 9.80 \times 0.360} = \boxed{19.9\ m/s}\)
(b) \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{165 \times 9.80 \times 0.360} = \boxed{24.2\ m/s}\)
Problem 3

A car (mass 1200 kg) rounds a flat curve of radius 80 m. (a) If the coefficient of static friction is \(\mu_s = 0.65\), what is the maximum speed? (b) What centripetal force does friction provide at this speed? (c) If the road is wet (\(\mu_s = 0.30\)), what is the new max speed?

(a) \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r} = \sqrt{0.65 \times 9.80 \times 80} = \boxed{22.6\ m/s}\) (≈ 81 km/h)
(b) \(F_c = \mu_s mg = 0.65 \times 1200 \times 9.80 = \boxed{7644\ N}\)
(c) \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{0.30 \times 9.80 \times 80} = \boxed{15.3\ m/s}\) (≈ 55 km/h) — much slower on wet roads!
Problem 4

A ball on a string (mass 0.50 kg, string length 1.2 m) swings in a horizontal circle. (a) If the speed is 4.0 m/s, what is the tension in the string? (b) The string can hold a maximum tension of 50 N. What is the maximum speed?

(a) \(T = mv^2/r = 0.50 \times (4.0)^2 / 1.2 = \boxed{6.67\ N}\)
(b) \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{T_{\max} \cdot r / m} = \sqrt{50 \times 1.2 / 0.50} = \boxed{10.95\ m/s}\)
