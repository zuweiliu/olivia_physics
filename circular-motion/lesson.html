Section 1 of 6 — Core Concepts

Circular Motion
Kinematics

In previous chapters, you studied straight-line motion (displacement, velocity, acceleration) and projectile motion (2D, but the object lands). Now we study objects that don't land — they move in circles. Think of a car rounding a curve, a planet orbiting a star, or a point on a spinning CD.

🎮 Watch: a point moves in a circle. The green arrow is velocity (tangent), the gold arrow is the angle swept.

The first Ferris wheel (1893, Chicago) was 80 m in diameter — riders at the rim were in uniform circular motion, constantly accelerating toward the center even though their speed never changed!

💡 Big Idea #1 — Rotational Angle (Angular Displacement)

Just like distance tells you how far something moved in a line, angular displacement \(\theta\) tells you how much something has rotated.

Where Does \(\theta = \dfrac{s}{r}\) Come From?

Imagine a circle of radius \(r\). A point on the circle moves along an arc of length \(s\). The angle swept out is defined as:

\[ \theta = \frac{s}{r} \]

Physics meaning: \(\theta\) tells you what fraction of the full circle you've swept through. A full circle has arc length \(s = 2\pi r\), so \(\theta = 2\pi r / r = 2\pi\) radians = 360°.

⚠️ Why radians, not degrees?

Radians make the formula beautifully simple: \(s = r\theta\). No conversion factors needed. In degrees you'd need \(s = \frac{\pi r \theta°}{180}\) — messy! Physics always uses radians.

🔍 Formula Analysis: \(\theta = s / r\)

VariableRolePhysics Meaning
\(\theta\)VariesHow much the object has rotated (rad)
\(s\)VariesArc length traveled (m)
\(r\)Constant for a given pointDistance from the center (m)

Key insight: Two points on the same spinning disk sweep through the same angle \(\theta\), but the outer point travels a longer arc because its \(r\) is larger: \(s = r\theta\).

💡 Big Idea #2 — Angular Velocity \(\omega\)

Just like velocity tells you how fast position changes, angular velocity \(\omega\) tells you how fast the angle changes.

Angular Velocity: \(\omega = \dfrac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}\)

If an object sweeps through angle \(\Delta\theta\) in time \(\Delta t\):

\[ \omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} \quad \text{(rad/s)} \]

The Golden Bridge: \(v = r\omega\)

This connects linear speed (m/s) and angular speed (rad/s):

\[ v = r\,\omega \]

Where it comes from: Start with \(s = r\theta\). Divide both sides by \(\Delta t\): \(\frac{s}{\Delta t} = r \cdot \frac{\theta}{\Delta t}\), which gives \(v = r\omega\).

🔍 Formula Analysis: \(v = r\omega\)

VariableRoleIf it changes…
\(v\)Linear speed (m/s)—
\(r\)Radius — constant for a given pointLarger \(r\) → faster \(v\) at same \(\omega\)
\(\omega\)Angular speed (rad/s)Spin faster → bigger \(v\)
✅ Same disk, different speeds!

Every point on a spinning CD has the same \(\omega\), but points near the edge have a larger \(v\) because they have a larger \(r\). That's why the outer track of a CD stores more data per rotation!

💡 Big Idea #3 — Period \(T\) and Frequency \(f\)

Period = time for one full revolution. Frequency = how many revolutions per second. They are reciprocals: \(T = 1/f\).

Connecting Everything

One full revolution sweeps \(\theta = 2\pi\) radians in time \(T\):

\[ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f \]

The linear speed for one full circle (circumference \(= 2\pi r\)) in time \(T\):

\[ v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = 2\pi r f \]

📋 The Complete Relationship Map

\[ \boxed{ \theta = \frac{s}{r}, \quad \omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f, \quad v = r\omega = \frac{2\pi r}{T} } \]

💡 Big Idea #4 — Centripetal Acceleration

Even at constant speed, a circling object is always accelerating — because its direction keeps changing. This acceleration points toward the center.

Centripetal Acceleration

Acceleration means a change in velocity — and velocity is a vector (speed + direction). In uniform circular motion, the speed stays the same but the direction constantly changes. The resulting acceleration always points inward, toward the center.

\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r \]

"Centripetal" = Latin for "center-seeking." It's not a new type of acceleration — it's just the name for acceleration that points toward the center of a circle.

🔍 Formula Analysis: \(a_c = v^2/r\)

VariableRolePhysics Meaning of Change
\(a_c\)Centripetal acceleration (m/s²)How sharply the direction changes
\(v\)Speed — can vary or be constantDouble \(v\) → 4× the acceleration!
\(r\)Radius — constant for a given pathTighter curve (smaller \(r\)) → more acceleration
Driving analogy
Faster car on same curve → you feel pushed harder sideways. That's \(v^2\) in the numerator!
Tighter curve
Same speed but sharper turn → more acceleration. That's \(r\) in the denominator!
⚠️ Alternate form: \(a_c = \omega^2 r\)

Substitute \(v = r\omega\) into \(a_c = v^2/r\) to get \(a_c = \omega^2 r\). Now larger \(r\) means more acceleration (at the same \(\omega\)), which makes sense — the outer edge of a merry-go-round flings you harder!

Section 3 of 6 — Worked Example

Example: The Ferris Wheel 🎡

Problem Statement

A Ferris wheel has a radius of 38 m. The carts at the rim move at a constant speed of 1.8 m/s.

(a) What is the angular velocity of a cart at the rim?
(b) What is the period of one full revolution?
(c) What is the centripetal acceleration at the rim?
(d) A cart is placed halfway from the center (r = 19 m). What are its \(v\) and \(\omega\)?

Work through each step by clicking the buttons below 👇

1
Part (a): Angular velocity at the rim

Use \(v = r\omega\), solve for \(\omega\)

\[ \omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{1.8\ m/s}{38\ m} = \boxed{0.0474\ rad/s} \]

💡 That's a slow spin — about 0.45 RPM. Makes sense for a Ferris wheel!

2
Part (b): Period of revolution

Use \(\omega = 2\pi/T\), solve for \(T\)

\[ T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{0.0474} = \boxed{132.6\ s \approx 2.2\ min} \]

Or directly: \(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi(38)}{1.8} = 132.6\) s.

3
Part (c): Centripetal acceleration at the rim

Use \(a_c = v^2/r\)

\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(1.8)^2}{38} = \frac{3.24}{38} = \boxed{0.0853\ m/s^2} \]
✅ Compare to gravity: \(a_c / g = 0.0853 / 9.80 = 0.0087g\)

Less than 1% of g — you'd barely feel it! That's why Ferris wheels feel gentle.

4
Part (d): Cart halfway from center

Same \(\omega\), but different \(r\) → different \(v\)

\[ \omega_{\text{half}} = \omega_{\text{rim}} = 0.0474\ rad/s \quad \text{(same — rigid body!)} \] \[ v_{\text{half}} = r_{\text{half}} \cdot \omega = 19 \times 0.0474 = \boxed{0.90\ m/s} \]
🔑 Key Insight:

\(\omega\) is the same for every point on a rigid rotating body. But \(v\) is different — halving the radius halves the linear speed! This is the power of \(v = r\omega\).

Homework Problems 📝

📋 Key Formulas Reference

\(\theta = s/r\)  |  \(\omega = \Delta\theta / \Delta t = 2\pi/T = 2\pi f\)  |  \(v = r\omega = 2\pi r / T\)  |  \(a_c = v^2/r = \omega^2 r\)

Problem 1

A car follows a curve of radius 500 m at a speed of 25.0 m/s (about 90 km/h). (a) What is the centripetal acceleration? (b) Compare it with \(g\).

(a) \(a_c = v^2/r = (25.0)^2/500 = 625/500 = \boxed{1.25\ m/s^2}\)
(b) \(a_c/g = 1.25/9.80 = 0.128\). The centripetal acceleration is about 13% of g — noticeable in a car but not extreme.
Problem 2

A CD spins at 500 RPM (revolutions per minute). (a) What is the angular velocity in rad/s? (b) A point at \(r = 6.0\) cm from the center — what is its linear speed? (c) A point at the edge, \(r = 6.0\) cm — what is its centripetal acceleration?

(a) \(\omega = 500 \times 2\pi/60 = \boxed{52.4\ rad/s}\)
(b) \(v = r\omega = 0.060 \times 52.4 = \boxed{3.14\ m/s}\)
(c) \(a_c = \omega^2 r = (52.4)^2(0.060) = \boxed{164.7\ m/s^2}\) — that's about 16.8g!
Problem 3

Earth orbits the Sun with a period of approximately 365.25 days at a mean radius of \(1.50 \times 10^{11}\) m. (a) What is Earth's angular velocity? (b) What is Earth's orbital speed? (c) What is the centripetal acceleration toward the Sun?

(a) \(T = 365.25 \times 24 \times 3600 = 3.156 \times 10^7\ s\).
\(\omega = 2\pi / T = \boxed{1.99 \times 10^{-7}\ rad/s}\)
(b) \(v = r\omega = 1.50\!\times\!10^{11} \times 1.99\!\times\!10^{-7} = \boxed{2.99 \times 10^4\ m/s}\) ≈ 30 km/s!
(c) \(a_c = v^2/r = (2.99\!\times\!10^4)^2 / (1.50\!\times\!10^{11}) = \boxed{5.95 \times 10^{-3}\ m/s^2}\)
Problem 4

A bicycle wheel has a radius of 0.33 m and is spinning at \(\omega = 12\) rad/s. (a) What is the linear speed of a point on the rim? (b) Through what angle (in radians) does the wheel turn in 5.0 seconds? (c) What arc length does a point on the rim travel in 5.0 s?

(a) \(v = r\omega = 0.33 \times 12 = \boxed{3.96\ m/s}\)
(b) \(\theta = \omega \cdot t = 12 \times 5.0 = \boxed{60\ rad}\) (about 9.5 revolutions)
(c) \(s = r\theta = 0.33 \times 60 = \boxed{19.8\ m}\)
