Kepler's Laws of
Planetary Motion 🪐

Over 400 years ago, a brilliant astronomer named Johannes Kepler cracked the cosmic code of how planets move — without a telescope, without a computer, just mountains of hand-written data and pure determination. His three laws describe every orbit in the universe: from Mercury to Mars, from satellites to the Moon, even the paths of comets! ☄️

Kepler spent 8 years trying to fit Mars's orbit into a perfect circle before finally realizing it was an ellipse. He said the answer was so unexpected that he initially thought he had made a calculation error! Talk about persistence. 💪

Kepler's 1st Law

The Law of Ellipses

Every planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus.

✨ What's an Ellipse?

An ellipse is like a stretched circle — an oval shape with two special points called foci (singular: focus). Drag the slider to see how the shape changes!

Shape: ⭕ circle 🥚 elongated

Eccentricity measures how "squished" the ellipse is:   \(\displaystyle e = \frac{c}{a}\)

\(e = 0\) → perfect circle  |  \(e\) close to 1 → very elongated  |  also: \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\)

⚠️ Key Point:

A circle is a special ellipse with eccentricity \(e = 0\) (both foci merge into the center). Earth's orbit is nearly circular (\(e = 0.017\)), while comets can have very elongated orbits (\(e \gt 0.9\))! The Sun sits at one focus — not the center.

Kepler's 2nd Law

The Law of Equal Areas

A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals.

🏃‍♀️ What Does This Mean?

Imagine a line connecting the Sun to the planet. As the planet orbits, this line sweeps out a pie-slice shape. Kepler discovered that every slice of the same time duration has the same area! Look at the diagram — both shaded wedges cover the same amount of time:

Both colored wedges represent the same time interval — notice they have equal area!

🚀

Short radius but wide angle
Planet moves FAST to sweep the same area
🐢
Near Aphelion (far)
Long radius but narrow angle
Planet moves SLOW — long arm makes up for it
🧠 Think of it like an ice skater!

When a figure skater pulls their arms in, they spin faster. When they stretch out, they spin slower. The planet does the same thing — closer to the Sun (arms in) → faster. Farther away (arms out) → slower. Same total "sweep" each second!

Earth is closest to the Sun in January (perihelion) and farthest in July (aphelion). Surprise — we're actually closer to the Sun during the Northern Hemisphere's winter! ❄️☀️ Seasons are caused by Earth's tilt, not its distance from the Sun.
Kepler's 3rd Law

The Law of Harmonies

The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis:

\[ T^2 \propto r^3 \]

🔗 Connecting to Newton's Gravity

Kepler discovered the pattern, but Newton explained WHY using gravity! Let's derive the exact relationship for circular orbits (where \(a = r\)):

1
Start with the satellite speed formula
\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} \]
2
Speed = distance / time for one orbit
\[ v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} \]
3
Set them equal and square both sides
\[ \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{r} \]
4
Rearrange to isolate \(T^2\)
\[ T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,r^3 \]

🤯 \(T^2/r^3\) is the SAME constant for every object orbiting the same central body!

🏆 KEPLER'S THIRD LAW (Newton's Form)

\[ \boxed{T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,r^3} \]

where \(M\) = central body mass, \(r\) = orbital radius, \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ N \cdot m^2/kg^2\)

📜 Kepler's Story — A Cosmic Detective

1571
Born in Germany — premature, sickly, with poor eyesight. Ironic for someone who would study the skies!
1600
Joins Tycho Brahe — the greatest naked-eye observer ever, who had decades of incredibly precise planetary data.
1601
Tycho dies — Kepler inherits all his data. The treasure hunt begins! 🗺️
1609
Laws 1 & 2 published — after 8 years of calculations by hand, he cracks the elliptical orbit code.
1619
Law 3 published — 10 more years to find the \(T^2 \propto r^3\) pattern. Kepler called it "harmonies of the world." 🎵

Section 3 of 6 — Worked Example

Example: Mars's Orbit 🔴

Problem Statement

Mars orbits the Sun at an average distance of 2.28 × 10¹¹ m (1.52 AU).

(a) What is Mars's orbital period in seconds?
(b) Convert the period to Earth days.
(c) Verify using Kepler's 3rd law ratio method: if Earth's period is 1 year at 1 AU, what is Mars's period?

Work through each step by clicking the buttons below 👇

1
Identify known values

Gather the data we need

\[ r_{Mars} = 2.28 \times 10^{11}\ m \] \[ M_{Sun} = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ kg \] \[ G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ N\cdot m^2/kg^2 \]

💡 For planets orbiting the Sun, \(M\) is always the Sun's mass!

2
Part (a): Apply Kepler's 3rd Law

\(T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/(GM)}\)

\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM_{Sun}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(2.28 \times 10^{11})^3}{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30}}} \] \[ = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1.186 \times 10^{34}}{1.328 \times 10^{20}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{8.932 \times 10^{13}} \] \[ \boxed{T \approx 5.93 \times 10^{7}\ s} \]
3
Part (b): Convert to days

Divide by seconds per day

\[ T_{days} = \frac{5.93 \times 10^7\ s}{86{,}400\ s/day} \approx \boxed{687\ \text{days}} \]
✅ Real-world check: NASA's value for Mars's orbital period is 687 days. Spot on!
4
Part (c): The elegant ratio method!

Compare Mars to Earth using \(T^2 \propto r^3\)

\[ \frac{T_{Mars}^2}{T_{Earth}^2} = \frac{r_{Mars}^3}{r_{Earth}^3} \] \[ T_{Mars}^2 = T_{Earth}^2 \times \left(\frac{r_{Mars}}{r_{Earth}}\right)^3 = (1\ \text{yr})^2 \times (1.52)^3 \] \[ T_{Mars}^2 = 3.512 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boxed{T_{Mars} = 1.874\ \text{years} \approx 684\ \text{days}} \]

🎯 The ratio method is super elegant — you don't even need \(G\) or \(M_{Sun}\)! Just compare to Earth.

🤔 Thinking Question

If a new planet were discovered at 4 AU from the Sun, would its year be (a) 4 years, (b) 8 years, or (c) some other value?

8 years! \(T^2 = r^3 = 4^3 = 64\), so \(T = \sqrt{64} = 8\) years. The period doesn't scale linearly with distance — it grows faster! That's why Neptune at 30 AU takes 165 years, not 30 years. 🤯

(a) Using the ratio method with Earth, find Jupiter's orbital period in years.

Use \(T^2 = r^3\) when distances are in AU and periods in years.

Part (b): Convert orbital distance to meters

Multiply: \(r = 5.20\ \text{AU} \times 1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m/AU}\)

Part (c): Calculate \(T\) using the full formula

Use \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/(GM_{Sun})}\) and convert to years (÷ 3.156×10⁷ s/yr).

Group A — Kepler's 3rd Law Calculations

Problem 1

Saturn orbits the Sun at an average distance of 9.54 AU. Using the ratio method (compare to Earth), find Saturn's orbital period in years.

\(T^2 = r^3 = (9.54)^3 = 867.4\)
\(T = \sqrt{867.4} \approx \boxed{29.5\ \text{years}}\)
Problem 2

An asteroid has an orbital period of 8 years around the Sun. What is its average distance from the Sun in AU?

\(r^3 = T^2 = 8^2 = 64\)
\(r = 64^{1/3} = \boxed{4.00\ \text{AU}}\)
That's between Mars (1.52 AU) and Jupiter (5.20 AU) — right in the asteroid belt!
Problem 3

A moon orbits Jupiter with a period of 3.55 days (Io) at a distance of 4.22 × 10⁸ m. Another moon (Europa) orbits at 6.71 × 10⁸ m. Find Europa's orbital period in days.

Using ratios: \(\frac{T_E^2}{T_I^2} = \frac{r_E^3}{r_I^3}\)
\(T_E^2 = (3.55)^2 \times \left(\frac{6.71 \times 10^8}{4.22 \times 10^8}\right)^3 = 12.60 \times (1.590)^3 = 12.60 \times 4.021 = 50.66\)
\(T_E = \sqrt{50.66} \approx \boxed{7.12\ \text{days}}\)
NASA's value: 7.15 days — very close!

Group B — Conceptual & Challenge

Problem 4

A comet has an elliptical orbit around the Sun with a semi-major axis of 18 AU. (a) What is the comet's orbital period? (b) If the comet was last at perihelion (closest to the Sun) in 2020, when will it return?

(a) \(T^2 = 18^3 = 5832 \Rightarrow T = \sqrt{5832} \approx \boxed{76.4\ \text{years}}\)
(b) Next perihelion: \(2020 + 76.4 \approx \boxed{2096}\)
Fun fact: Halley's Comet has a similar period (~76 years)! ☄️
Problem 5

Scientists discover an exoplanet orbiting a star that has twice the mass of our Sun. The planet is at a distance of 1 AU from this star. (a) Is its period longer, shorter, or the same as Earth's? (b) Calculate the period using \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/(GM)}\).

(a) Shorter — bigger \(M\) in the denominator means smaller \(T\).
(b) \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(1.496\times10^{11})^3}{6.674\times10^{-11}\times2(1.99\times10^{30})}}\)
\(= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3.348\times10^{33}}{2.655\times10^{20}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{1.261\times10^{13}}\)
\(\approx 2.23\times10^7\ s \approx \boxed{0.707\ \text{years} \approx 258\ \text{days}}\)
That's \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of Earth's year — makes sense since \(M\) doubled!
Problem 6 (Challenge)

Use Kepler's 3rd Law to find the mass of the Sun! You know that Earth orbits at \(r = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\) m with a period of \(T = 3.156 \times 10^7\) s. Rearrange \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3\) to solve for \(M\).

\[M = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GT^2} = \frac{4\pi^2 (1.496\times10^{11})^3}{6.674\times10^{-11}\times(3.156\times10^7)^2}\] \[= \frac{4\pi^2 \times 3.348\times10^{33}}{6.674\times10^{-11}\times9.960\times10^{14}} = \frac{1.322\times10^{35}}{6.647\times10^{4}}\] \[\boxed{M_{Sun} \approx 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ kg}\] This is how astronomers actually measure the mass of stars — by watching their planets orbit! 🌟🔭
