💡 Step 1: Review — Circular Motion

Before we calculate satellite speed, we need to remember how circular motion works. Any object moving in a circle needs a force pulling it toward the center!

Circular Motion Essentials

When an object moves in a circle at constant speed, it's constantly changing direction. That means it's accelerating — even if the speed stays the same!

🎯
Centripetal Acceleration
\(a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r}\)
💪
Centripetal Force
\(F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\)
⏱️
Speed from Period
\(v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}\)
⚠️ Key Point:

The centripetal force is NOT a new force — it's whatever real force is pulling the object toward the center. For a ball on a string, it's tension. For a car turning, it's friction. For a satellite, it's gravity!

💡 Step 2: Review — Universal Gravitation

Gravity is the force that pulls the satellite toward Earth — and it's what provides the centripetal force!

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

The gravitational force between a planet of mass \(M\) and a satellite of mass \(m\), separated by distance \(r\):

\[ F_g = G \frac{Mm}{r^2} \]

Remember: \(r\) is measured from center-to-center, not from the surface!

🚀 Step 3: Deriving Satellite Speed!

Now the magic: set gravity equal to the centripetal force, and watch the satellite's mass cancel out!

The Derivation

For a satellite in circular orbit, gravity IS the centripetal force:

1
Set gravitational force = centripetal force
\[ G\frac{Mm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]
2
Cancel the satellite mass \(m\) from both sides!
\[ G\frac{M\cancel{m}}{r^2} = \frac{\cancel{m}\,v^2}{r} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G\frac{M}{r^2} = \frac{v^2}{r} \]

🤯 The satellite's mass doesn't matter! A bowling ball and a feather orbit at the same speed!

3
Multiply both sides by \(r\) and solve for \(v\)
\[ v^2 = \frac{GM}{r} \]

🏆 THE SATELLITE SPEED FORMULA

\[ \boxed{v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}} \]

where \(M\) = planet mass, \(r\) = orbital radius (center to center), \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ N \cdot m^2/kg^2\)

Since the satellite's mass cancels, every satellite at the same altitude orbits at the same speed — whether it's a 1 kg cubesat or the 420,000 kg ISS!

Bonus: Orbital Period

Since speed = distance / time, and one full orbit has distance \(2\pi r\):

\[ T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 2\pi r \sqrt{\frac{r}{GM}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}} \]

Higher orbit → bigger \(r\) → slower speed and longer period. At 35,786 km altitude, \(T\) = 24 hours — that's a geostationary satellite! 📡

Section 3 of 6 — Worked Example

Example: The ISS 🛰️

Problem Statement

The International Space Station orbits Earth at an altitude of 408 km above the surface.

(a) What is its orbital speed?
(b) How long does one orbit take (the period)?
(c) How many orbits does it complete per day?

Work through each step by clicking the buttons below 👇

1
Find the orbital radius \(r\)

r = Earth's radius + altitude

\[ r = r_E + h = 6.37 \times 10^6 + 408 \times 10^3 \] \[ \boxed{r = 6.778 \times 10^6\ m} \]

💡 Always convert altitude to meters and add Earth's radius!

2
Part (a): Apply the satellite speed formula

\(v = \sqrt{GM_E / r}\)

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.98 \times 10^{24}}{6.778 \times 10^6}} \] \[ = \sqrt{\frac{3.991 \times 10^{14}}{6.778 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.888 \times 10^7} \] \[ \boxed{v \approx 7{,}674\ m/s \approx 7.67\ km/s} \]

That's about 27,600 km/h — fast enough to cross the entire US in about 9 minutes! 🇺🇸

3
Part (b): Find the orbital period

\(T = 2\pi r / v\)

\[ T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 6.778 \times 10^6}{7674} \] \[ = \frac{4.259 \times 10^7}{7674} \approx 5{,}549\ s \] \[ \boxed{T \approx 5{,}549\ s \approx 92.5\ \text{minutes}} \]
4
Part (c): Orbits per day

Divide 24 hours by the period

\[ \text{Orbits per day} = \frac{24 \times 60\ \text{min}}{92.5\ \text{min}} \approx \boxed{15.6\ \text{orbits/day}} \]
✅ Real-world check: NASA confirms the ISS completes about 15.5 orbits per day. Our answer is spot on!

Astronauts experience 15–16 sunrises every 24 hours! 🌅🌅🌅

🤔 Thinking Question

If we doubled the mass of the ISS (by adding more modules), would it need to go faster, slower, or the same speed?

The same speed! Remember, the satellite's mass \(m\) canceled out in the derivation. The orbital speed \(v = \sqrt{GM/r}\) depends only on the planet's mass and the orbital radius. A heavier ISS orbits at the same speed as a lighter one! 🤯

Group A — Satellite Speed Calculations

Problem 1

The Hubble Space Telescope orbits at an altitude of 547 km above Earth's surface. Find its (a) orbital speed and (b) orbital period in minutes.

(a) \(r = 6.37\!\times\!10^6 + 547\!\times\!10^3 = 6.917\!\times\!10^6\) m
\(v = \sqrt{GM_E/r} = \sqrt{6.674\!\times\!10^{-11}\!\times\!5.98\!\times\!10^{24}/6.917\!\times\!10^6} \approx \boxed{7{,}594\ m/s}\)
(b) \(T = 2\pi r/v = 2\pi(6.917\!\times\!10^6)/7594 \approx 5723\ s \approx \boxed{95.4\ \text{min}}\)
Problem 2

A geostationary satellite has an orbital period of exactly 24 hours. Find (a) its orbital radius and (b) its altitude above Earth's surface in km.

(a) From \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/(GM_E)}\), solve for \(r\):
\(r = \left(\dfrac{GM_E T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\dfrac{6.674\!\times\!10^{-11}\times5.98\!\times\!10^{24}\times(86400)^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} \approx \boxed{4.23\times10^7\ m}\)
(b) altitude \(= r - r_E = 4.23\!\times\!10^7 - 6.37\!\times\!10^6 \approx \boxed{35{,}900\ km}\)
Problem 3

A satellite orbits Mars at an altitude of 300 km. Mars has mass \(6.42 \times 10^{23}\) kg and radius \(3.39 \times 10^6\) m. Find its orbital speed and period.

\(r = 3.39\!\times\!10^6 + 300\!\times\!10^3 = 3.69\!\times\!10^6\) m
\(v = \sqrt{6.674\!\times\!10^{-11}\times6.42\!\times\!10^{23}/3.69\!\times\!10^6} \approx \boxed{3{,}407\ m/s}\)
\(T = 2\pi(3.69\!\times\!10^6)/3407 \approx 6802\ s \approx \boxed{113.4\ \text{min}}\)

Group B — Conceptual & Challenge

Problem 4

Two satellites orbit Earth: Satellite A at altitude 400 km, Satellite B at altitude 800 km. (a) Which is faster? (b) Find the ratio \(v_A / v_B\). (c) Which has a shorter period?

(a) Satellite A is faster (lower orbit → higher speed).
(b) \(v_A/v_B = \sqrt{r_B/r_A} = \sqrt{7.17\!\times\!10^6/6.77\!\times\!10^6} \approx \boxed{1.029}\)
(c) Satellite A has a shorter period (lower orbit → faster → shorter T).
Problem 5

A spy satellite orbits at the lowest stable altitude, about 160 km. (a) Find its speed. (b) How many times does it orbit Earth per day? (c) How long does it spend over any single point on the ground (assume it "sees" a 500 km strip)?

(a) \(r=6.53\!\times\!10^6\) m, \(v=\sqrt{GM_E/r}\approx\boxed{7{,}814\ m/s}\)
(b) \(T=2\pi r/v\approx5253\ s\approx87.6\) min → \(\boxed{16.4\ \text{orbits/day}}\)
(c) Time over a 500 km strip \(\approx 500\!\times\!10^3/7814\approx\boxed{64\ s}\) — barely a minute!
Problem 6 (Challenge)

Derive an expression for the orbital speed of a satellite orbiting any planet in terms of the planet's surface gravity \(g_p\) and the planet's radius \(R_p\), for a satellite at altitude \(h\). Hint: recall that \(g_p = GM_p/R_p^2\).

Since \(GM_p = g_p R_p^2\) and \(r = R_p + h\):
\[\boxed{v = R_p\sqrt{\frac{g_p}{R_p + h}}}\] This is useful because you only need \(g_p\), \(R_p\), and \(h\) — no need to look up \(G\) or \(M_p\)!
